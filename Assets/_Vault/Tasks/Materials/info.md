Let me back up and demystify the mask first, because it's simpler than it sounds — then answer your actual proposal, which does work but has one catch worth understanding.
What a mask actually is: it's just a second image, the same size and lined up with your shirt, that you use as a label map instead of something you look at. Where the shirt fabric is, you paint that second image pure red. Where the buttons are, you paint it pure green. It's not art — it's you coloring inside the lines to tell the shader "these pixels are fabric, those pixels are buttons." That's the whole idea. The shader looks at the label to know which pixels get the shirt color and which get the button color.
Now your proposal:
"Just replace each gray value with a chosen color." Yes, this works, and it's a real, common technique. It's actually the palette approach, keyed on the gray value itself. The mental model: your buttons are painted one specific gray (say value 100), your fabric another (say value 200). You hand the shader a little table: "gray 100 → gold, gray 200 → blue." Every pixel looks up its gray value in the table and becomes that color. Swap the table and you get red fabric, brown buttons. No second image needed.
Here's the catch, and it's the thing that decides whether this route is right for you: a shirt isn't one flat gray. A blue shirt in a game has folds, shadows, highlights — so the fabric is really gray 180 in the shadow, 200 in the midtone, 230 on a highlight. If your table only says "200 → blue," then the shadows and highlights don't match any entry and don't get colored. So a pure "this exact gray → this color" swap only works cleanly when each region is a single flat gray with no shading baked in. That's exactly why this technique lives in flat/cel and pixel-art styles — every zone is one solid color, so one gray = one region = one lookup. It's clean and it's easy to author.
If you want folds and soft shading, you don't abandon your idea — you split the gray into two jobs. Reserve a small set of flat "ID" grays to mark which region a pixel is (fabric vs buttons vs collar), and let a separate gentle shading layer carry the light and dark. The lookup picks the base color from the ID gray, then you multiply the shading on top so folds survive. That "which region" gray is the mask, just packed into your one image instead of a second one — so you've reinvented the mask, which is why I reached for it. It's the same tool wearing different clothes.
So the honest tradeoff:

Flat/cel or pixel style: your exact idea works perfectly as-is. One gray per region, a small color table per part, done. Simplest possible thing, and it's a great fit.
Soft painted shading with folds: the plain "gray → color" swap will flatten your shading, and you'll end up needing region IDs + a shading pass, which is the mask by another name.

That's why I keep asking about your art style — it's not pedantry, it genuinely picks the technique. First correction: you're not adding more textures per variation. You bake each part into the atlas exactly once, at one size, in one neutral color. The thousand color variations don't live in the atlas — they live in a per-instance tint value you attach to each quad entity. Same atlas pixels, different tint number per instance. So tinting doesn't grow your atlas; it's the thing that stops your atlas from growing. Your "atlas is full of variations" problem exists precisely because variations are currently baked instead of tinted. Bake once, tint many.
Now the real question — outlines. The trick is how you bake the part and which blend you use. Bake each part as white (or light-gray) fill with the black outline baked right into the same sprite. Then tint with a multiply, not a replace:
result = spritePixel * tint
Watch what multiply does to each pixel:

Fill baked white (1,1,1) → 1 * tint = tint. Becomes your chosen color. ✅
Outline baked black (0,0,0) → 0 * tint = 0. Stays black, always. ✅
Light shading baked as 0.85 gray → 0.85 * tint = a slightly darker shade of the tint. Your subtle folds survive, proportionally. ✅

That's the whole answer to "how do I tint them with outlines": black is immune to multiply because zero times anything is zero. You don't have to protect the outline or exclude it — the math protects it for free. Bake the outline black, multiply-tint the sprite, and the fill recolors while the outline stays a crisp constant black. This is the single cleanest path for your exact art style, and it's basically free in a shader.
For a part with one color region (pants, a hat), that's it — done.

Split by region into separate quads — shirt quad and buttons quad, each baked white-fill-black-outline, each with its own multiply tint. Fits your existing 30-quad assembly model directly, no shader beyond multiply. Outlines on both stay black automatically.
Or one quad + a mask to route different tints to fabric vs buttons in a single sprite. Fewer quads, one extra texture sample. And the outline, baked black, still survives untouched under both tints.
